Here's a question to hold onto as we go: how do you get an agent to design something *with* you, without it quietly designing something else? My answer turned out to be a string of small steps, each one refusing to be skipped. Let's walk through them with <Name />, one at a time.

Every figure on this page is built from the files the [operator-design](https://github.com/thoughtgrain/operator-design) MCP server wrote while the session ran: the briefs, the drawings, the measurements, the decisions and the times they were made. The buttons don't change what happened. They let you look at it from the angles the process did.

## Does it even need a screen?

The very first question is whether to draw anything at all. Every OP-XY machine built with the [open-xy SDK](https://github.com/etcetc000/open-xy) gets a stock page for free: a header with its four control names and their values. For plenty of machines, that's all you need. A custom screen has to earn its place by having one thing the sound acts on, something you could point at.

Flip between the two and ask yourself which one tells you more about the sound.

<StockOrScreen />

For <Name />, the brief answers in one sentence: <Field name="subject" />. If I can't write that sentence, the machine keeps the stock page and the protocol ends right there. That sounds like giving up, and it's the opposite. A screen that can't name its subject is worse than no screen.

## Writing down what it sounds like

Before anyone draws, the machine gets described in words: what it is, what it sounds like, which controls it has and where they came from. Then the server runs its definition through the same rules [open-xy](https://github.com/etcetc000/open-xy) will enforce, so nothing gets designed on top of a control that's about to change.

<Intake />

This is the paperwork step, and I've learned not to skip it. Every later step leans on it. The drawing has to be *of* this sound, and the knobs it shows have to be *these* knobs.

## How much does each knob matter?

Here's the step that surprises people, because it's about listening rather than looking. Each control gets a score from one to five for how much it changes the sound across its whole range.

Why would a drawing care? Because later, every knob has to move something on screen, and *how far* it moves is set by this score. A knob that barely changes the sound shouldn't make the picture leap. A knob that transforms it shouldn't twitch. The score turns "proportional" from a feeling into a number.

Pick a control to read what it does to the sound, and what its score commits the drawing to.

## Five directions, before a single line

Now the drawing starts. Well, almost. First, `design_pass_start` writes five briefs.

Each brief pairs a different angle on what the machine *is* (its mechanism, its material, a scene it lives in, a cutaway of it at work) with a different way of drawing. The five briefs go to five designer agents that work at the same time and never see each other's work.

Why fix the directions before anything is drawn? Because if you ask for five ideas and let them emerge, you get one idea and four echoes of it. I did that to myself for weeks. Handing out the directions first is the only way I've found to get five honest attempts.

Start with the briefs, then see what came back.

<Directions pass="first" />

## Are they really five?

Five drawings came back. But "five different drawings" is a claim, and the server checks it. `design_pass_submit` compares every pair, ten of them for five drawings, and refuses the pass if any two are too alike.

<Reached when="subjectPass" pass="first">

For a pass of subjects, a pair has to differ in at least two of three things: the angle, the way of drawing, and the wording of the subject. Wording is measured by counting the words two subjects share. Click any square to put that pair side by side, with their shared words highlighted.

</Reached>

<Reached when="framesPass" pass="first">

For a pass of frames, the check is pixels: any two rest frames have to differ on at least 4% of the screen's body. Click any square to put that pair side by side.

</Reached>

<Uniqueness pass="first" />

If a pair fails, the pass comes back as `needs_revision`, and the only fix is a new direction for the weaker drawing. Never dropping to four. That rule is the whole point. It's what stops "five ideas" from quietly becoming three.

## Who picks?

Not the server. Not the reviewer either. This is the part of the process I'm most stubborn about.

A reviewer agent reads all five and ranks them, with a reason for each. That ranking is useful, it's often right, and it's deliberately not a decision. The decision is the person's, and the server won't record one without a name and a reason attached.

## Tidying, and keeping

Sometimes a picked drawing needs small fixes: a stray label, a clipped edge, spacing that was off. That isn't a new idea, so it doesn't get five variants. It's the same drawing, cleaned, with a note saying exactly what changed. And now and then a variant that wasn't picked is worth keeping anyway, for another machine or as a reference.

## Seen as much as heard

With a subject chosen, the full set of frames gets drawn: the screen at rest, the screen while a note plays, and every control at 0, 50 and 100, with everything else left at its default.

This is where the scores come back. The server renders each control's frames and measures how much of the screen actually changes, against the floor that control's score set. Too little, and the control goes back a phase for more travel. The score never gets lowered to make the test pass.

## Everything at once

A slider is good for one control at a time. The contact sheet is for all of them together, and it's the check no tool can do for me: do the moving parts stay clear of each other at their extremes? It's judged at 1:1, because that's the only size this screen will ever be.

## Does it look like someone meant it?

Passing every check doesn't make a screen good. A drawing can clear every floor and still look like clip art. So before review, the rest frame goes beside a reference at 1:1 and gets asked a few blunt questions: is every element in one drawing language, is there exactly one brightest thing, does anything look like an icon?

## Still, until it isn't

Motion comes last, and it has one rule that shapes everything else: nothing moves at rest. An idle machine is a still picture. When a note arrives, the subject answers first, then settles, in less than a fifth of a second for anything a knob does.
